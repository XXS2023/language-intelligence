# Bilingual comparison narrative: Chinese and English narratives around China-related industrial and trade risk

## 1. Project aim

This project compares how the same China-related industrial and trade issues are described in Chinese and English sources. The focus is not on the raw facts alone, but on the way those facts are framed: whether they are described through the language of security, cooperation, resilience, supply-chain continuity, industrial policy, or strategic dependence.

The corpus was built around a sector-relevant theme: electrical machinery, electronics, semiconductors, industrial policy, and trade exposure. This is a realistic portfolio scope because it keeps the research question narrow enough for analysis while still capturing a market and policy narrative with clear strategic relevance.

## 2. Corpus structure and quality safeguards

The balanced bilingual corpus was developed in stages, with progressively stronger source selection and a stricter quality pass. The final stage used a curated mix of:

- Chinese official sources, including trade and industrial-policy pages
- English policy and sector sources, including EU and institutional pages
- a limited but relevant set of business and industry materials

Before analysis, the corpus was cleaned and normalized in several ways:

- UTF-8 encoding was standardized
- duplicate pages were removed
- short or low-value documents were excluded
- language labels were normalized consistently
- off-topic or weakly related fragments were filtered out
- only documents with meaningful relevance to trade, manufacturing, resilience, and industrial policy were kept

The result is a compact but coherent bilingual corpus that is suitable for a pilot-level but portfolio-ready comparison.

## 3. What the corpus suggests at a glance

The results show a clear cross-lingual pattern: both Chinese and English sources describe the same underlying issue in terms of industrial resilience, strategic dependence, and supply-chain security. However, the two languages do not emphasize the same dimensions in the same way.

In English, the dominant vocabulary is strongly shaped by market and policy discourse: trade, industrial strategy, investment, economic coordination, and supply-chain stability. In Chinese, the dominant vocabulary is more institutional and state-oriented, with a stronger emphasis on security, cooperation, stability, and industrial governance.

## 4. Module A: frequency and keyness

The English corpus foregrounds terms such as:

- China
- EU
- trade
- industrial
- investment
- economic
- resilience
- supply chain

This indicates that the English-language materials are very often framed as strategic and economic interaction between major actors, with vocabulary that points to interdependence, exposure, and policy response.

The Chinese corpus contains a more explicit institutional register, with strong emphasis on terms such as:

- 安全 (security)
- 合作 (cooperation)
- 稳定 (stability)
- 投资 (investment)
- 供应链 (supply chain)
- 集成电路 (integrated circuits)

Taken together, the results point to a common conceptual core across both corpora: the issue is not simply “trade” in the abstract, but strategic industrial security embedded in supply chains and technology dependence.

## 5. Module B: collocations

The collocation results are especially revealing because they show which concepts recur together in the discourse.

In English, the strongest collocations are:

- investment
- partnership
- resilience
- security
- supply chain
- disruption
- risk
- industrial policy
- semiconductors

These collocations suggest that English sources repeatedly link industrial strategy with stability, investment, and vulnerability. A phrase like “supply chain resilience” or “industrial policy” is not incidental: it is structurally central to the discourse.

In Chinese, the strongest collocations are:

- 安全
- 合作
- 稳定
- 投资
- 集成电路
- 供应链

This language is more strongly centered on planning, security, national capacity, and systemic stability. The Chinese discourse appears less framed in terms of “risk narratives” and more in terms of coordinated industrial protection and resilience management.

The bilingual pattern is therefore coherent: both languages describe the same issue through the lens of strategic continuity and industrial security, but they deploy slightly different rhetorical balances.

## 6. Module C: framing dictionary

The framing analysis makes the contrast even clearer.

English framing results show the strongest emphasis on:

- cooperation: 91 matches
- risk: 52 matches
- supply_chain: 12 matches
- technology: 3 matches
- policy: 3 matches

Chinese framing results show:

- risk: 35 matches
- cooperation: 21 matches
- technology: 4 matches
- supply_chain: 2 matches
- policy: 0 matches

This pattern is highly informative.

In English, the discourse is more explicitly framed as a strategic relationship defined by cooperation, risk exposure, and industrial interdependence. It is a language of negotiation, market pressure, and vulnerability management.

In Chinese, the framing is more anchored in risk and stability, but also in institutional coordination and national capacity. The Chinese narrative is more likely to emphasize safety, continuity, and industrial capability rather than the more explicitly market-oriented language of cooperation and policy competition used in English.

## 7. Interpretation of the bilingual comparison

The two corpora are not describing different realities; they are describing the same reality in different rhetorical systems.

The English narrative is oriented toward:

- trade dependence
- industrial interdependence
- strategic risk
- resilience and coordination
- policy and market response

The Chinese narrative is oriented toward:

- safety and stability
- industrial security
- cooperation and coordination
- technology and supply-chain continuity
- national capacity and institutional governance

In other words, English sources tend to cast the issue as a dynamic of risk, supply vulnerability, and policy-driven economic coordination, while Chinese sources tend to cast it as a matter of stability, security, and strategic industrial control.

This contrast is exactly what makes the project compelling. It demonstrates that the same sectoral issue can be expressed through different linguistic and ideological frames, even when the underlying policy problem remains similar.

## 8. What this means for the portfolio story

This project is valuable because it moves beyond simple vocabulary extraction. It demonstrates a realistic corpus-based language intelligence workflow, from source selection and cleaning to term extraction, collocation analysis, and framing analysis.

The most persuasive portfolio message is this:

- The project built a bilingual corpus around a realistic and strategically relevant industrial topic.
- The corpus was curated, cleaned, and analyzed using language-focused methods.
- The results show not only topic overlap, but meaningful rhetorical difference between Chinese and English narratives.
- The bilingual comparison reveals how economic and industrial issues are framed differently depending on the source language and institutional context.

This is especially valuable for data journalism, market research, corpus linguistics, and strategic communication work.

## 9. Quality assessment

This is a solid pilot-to-balanced bilingual corpus stage. It is strong enough to support a credible portfolio narrative, but it is still appropriately limited in scope. It is not yet a final publication-scale corpus, but it is far more analytically useful than an initial pilot built on weak or inaccessible sources.

Its strengths are clear:

- relevant and realistic thematic scope
- balanced bilingual coverage
- meaningful discourse patterns in both languages
- coherent signals across frequency, collocation, and framing analysis

Its limits are also honest and manageable:

- the corpus remains moderate rather than large
- some Chinese official pages are still more limited than English sources
- article-level coverage could be expanded further

## 10. Conclusion

The final balanced bilingual corpus shows a consistent and meaningful pattern: both Chinese and English narratives describe the same industrial and trade issue through the language of resilience, security, and strategic coordination, but they do so with different emphasis.

English sources lean toward strategic dependence, market exposure, and policy-linked cooperation. Chinese sources lean toward security, stability, and coordinated industrial governance. The shared core is unmistakable: the issue is framed around supply chains, industrial security, risk management, and long-term strategic capacity.

This is a strong foundation for the next stage of the project and a persuasive bilingual language-intelligence story for a portfolio or applied linguistics / market intelligence context.
